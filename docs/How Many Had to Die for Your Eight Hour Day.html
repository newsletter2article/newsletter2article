How Many Had to Die for Your Eight Hour Day?

The Collapse Chronicle
Sep 7
 
READ IN APP
 

“Labor was the first price, the original purchase-money that was paid for all things. It was not by gold or by silver, but by labor, that all wealth of the world was originally purchased.”

― Adam Smith.

Even though the eight-hour day seems a quaint historical artifact in the age of ubiquitous digital leashes, it remains a fundamental part of the social contract. For now.

America celebrates Labor Day on the first Monday of September. This tribute to American workers was purchased, quite literally, with blood and tireless effort. Few today recall how the first Labor Day came about, or that its real meaning lies beyond a day off and an opportunity to visit Walmart for “low low prices.” That forgetfulness is part of the point.

Many of the labor conditions that we accept as our birthright came at huge cost, won by working-class people banding together for better conditions. It’s easy to forget that the eight-hour day was such a radical, leftist idea that police would fire into crowds of workers to stop it. The campaign for an eight-hour day was a 19th-century labor movement that unfolded over decades.

International Workers’ Day (also known as May Day, or May 1) celebrates the international labor movement and marks the anniversary of the bloodiest struggles in labor history. Commemorated worldwide on May 1 with organized street demonstrations and marches by working people, it remains obscured by design in the United States. International Workers’ Day is the commemoration of the 1886 Haymarket Massacre in Chicago.

How it got moved to September in the US merits explanation.

After the Civil War, the United States experienced rapid industrial expansion. Chicago was a major industrial center, and tens of thousands of German and Bohemian immigrants were employed at pauper’s wages, about $1.50 a day. Not surprisingly, the city became a center for labor’s demands for better pay and working conditions. Employers responded with repression, including acts of violence, often abetted by police.

On May 1, 1886, Albert Parsons, head of the Chicago Knights of Labor, accompanied by 80,000 fellow workers, marched down Chicago’s Michigan Avenue, in what is regarded as the first modern May Day Parade. Every road in Chicago stopped running, and most industries in Chicago were paralyzed. The stockyards shut down. The state militia were called out, and the police were ready. In the next few days, they were joined nationwide by 350,000 workers who went on strike at 1,200 factories, including 70,000 in Chicago, 45,000 in New York, 32,000 in Cincinnati, and additional thousands in other cities. Some workers gained shorter hours (eight or nine) with no pay cut; others accepted pay cuts for reduced hours.

On May 3, 1886, August Spies, editor of the Arbeiter-Zeitung (Workers Newspaper), spoke at a meeting of 6,000 workers. Afterward, many of them moved down the street to harass strikebreakers at the McCormick plant in Chicago. At a subsequent rally on May 4 to protest this violence, a bomb exploded at Haymarket Square. The bomb wounded 66 police officers, of whom 7 later died. The police fired into the crowd, killing several people and wounding over 200. Hundreds of labor activists were rounded up and the prominent leaders arrested, tried, convicted, and executed, giving the movement its first martyrs.

Those put on trial were guilty only of their ideas. None of the accused had been at Haymarket that day except for one, who was speaking when the bomb exploded. A jury found them guilty, and they were sentenced to death. The Chicago Tribune even offered a bounty to the jurors if they would find the accused guilty. There was some evidence to suggest that the person who actually threw the bomb was an agent provocateur working for the police, hired to throw the bomb to enable the arrest of hundreds, and thus decapitate the revolutionary leadership. This was never proven. The immediate effect was to suppress the radical labor movement.

But the long-term effect was to fan the flames of class anger in many, and to inspire many others to action in revolutionary and labor causes. This effort would bear fruit in subsequent generations. Many thousands of people signed petitions, and a later governor of Illinois, John Peter Altgeld, investigated what happened at Haymarket and pardoned the three remaining prisoners who had not yet been executed. The real victims of Haymarket were freedom of speech, the right to free assembly, and the right to a fair and impartial trial by jury. As a result, workers’ right to organize and fight for issues like the eight-hour day was severely compromised.

The American Federation of Labor, meeting in St. Louis in December 1888, set May 1, 1890, as the day American workers should work no more than eight hours. The International Workingmen’s Association meeting in Paris in 1889 endorsed the date for international demonstrations, thus starting the international tradition of May Day.

May 1 remains International Workers Day worldwide, except in the United States, where the official workers' holiday is the first Monday in September. This is because Pres. Grover Cleveland feared that commemorating Labor Day on May 1 could become an opportunity to remember the Haymarket Massacre and thus create martyrs. So in 1894, Cleveland moved to support Labor Day in September, obscuring the focus on workers' rights. Right-wing governments have traditionally sought to repress the message behind International Workers Day, for obvious reasons.

Chicago designated the site of the Haymarket affair as a landmark in 1992, and the public sculpture was dedicated in 2004.

The lessons of history, including the lesson of International Workers’ Day or Labor Day, regardless of when celebrated, show that change ONLY happens when ordinary people band together with a common purpose to achieve their shared interests.
